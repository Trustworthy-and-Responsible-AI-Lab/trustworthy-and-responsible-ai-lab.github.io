Overview

AI systems are moving from passive language models to autonomous agents that plan, call tools, keep memory, read content they did not author, talk to other agents, and act on their users' behalf. That shift changes the question we ask of them. A model that writes a wrong sentence is a quality problem; an agent that sends the wrong email, runs the wrong shell command, or wires the wrong payment is a security problem.

This course studies the security of such systems from three perspectives: attacks, defenses, and human oversight. We start with a systems-level picture of how an agent is actually built, then ask what an adversary can do to each of its parts—reasoning, tools, memory, retrieved content, inter-agent messages, and delegated authority—which architectural and runtime mechanisms defend them, how to tell whether a published security claim holds up, and what a human operator must be shown in order to intervene in time.

We treat this as a systems and security problem rather than an extension of prompt engineering or adversarial machine learning. The course runs on recent research papers and a research-oriented final project. There are no labs and no weekly homework; the work is reading, threat modeling, security reasoning, and open-ended research.

In the end, we expect:

  • [Systems] To build a concrete mental model of how an AI agent actually works.
  • [Security] To learn how to think like an adversary who controls what an agent reads.
  • [Security] To learn attacks and defenses for agentic AI systems.
  • [Research] To learn how to pursue a research problem of your choice and communicate your findings with others.

Who Should Take This Course

This is a graduate research seminar. It requires a basic understanding of machine learning (e.g., CS 434). A prior course in security (e.g., CS 578) or in trustworthy ML (e.g., CS 499/599 | AI539) helps, but neither is required. You should be comfortable reading conference papers, and comfortable with the idea that a system's security depends on where its trust boundaries are drawn, not on how good its model is.

No programming assignments. You may still implement systems or run experiments as part of your final research project, and most strong projects do.

Course Policy

The University's Code of Academic Integrity applies, modified as follows:

[Don'ts]
  • Do NOT share your critique or write-up with others before the deadline.
  • Do NOT copy and paste someone else's text, figures, or code into yours.
  • Do NOT submit a critique that is a paraphrase of the paper's abstract, an existing public review, or a model-generated summary.
[Do's]
  • Brainstorm your ideas with other students.
  • Discuss and argue about the papers with other students—that is the point of the course.
  • Collaborate with your project partner on the design, experiments, and write-up.

Must: Please write down the names of students who helped you. It will not affect your score. You will learn from this practice how to credit others for their contributions—an essential skill when you collaborate later.

[Use of Generative AI]

This is a course about AI agents, so let us be precise about using them. Generative AI tools are permitted for the final research project (code, experiment scaffolding, literature search, and copy-editing) and for understanding background material. They are not permitted to produce the substance of your paper critiques or any part of the final exam.

Whenever you use such a tool on a graded artifact, you must disclose, in the submission itself: (1) which tool, (2) what kind of interaction (brainstorming, drafting, debugging, editing), and (3) which portion of the work it contributed to. This follows the EECS guidance on AI use policies. Undisclosed use is an academic integrity violation. You remain fully responsible for every claim in anything you submit, including claims a tool produced.
